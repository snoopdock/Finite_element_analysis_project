\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method (merged with The Finite Element Procedure)}

The Primal Finite Element Method is a foundation for direct discretization, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields. This approach is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems, as described by the equation \(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\) \cite{ref1}. However, primal methods face challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. The weak form, which is a crucial step in the discretization process, involves converting the governing partial differential equations into a weak (variational) form by multiplying with test functions and integrating over the domain, as shown in the equation \(\int_{\Omega} \alpha \nabla \Phi \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma} \sigma \Phi v \, d\Gamma = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\) \cite{ref3}.

The weak form of a partial differential equation (PDE) plays a pivotal role in the mathematical foundation of the finite element method. By converting the governing PDEs into a weak (variational) form through multiplication with test functions and integration over the domain, as described in the procedure of weak formulation \cite{ref3}, irregular geometries and discontinuous coefficients can be effectively handled. This weak form ensures compatibility with numerical discretization and enforces boundary conditions weakly, forming the basis for assembling the global stiffness matrix. For instance, the weak form of an elliptic PDE is given by \(\int_{\Omega} \alpha \nabla \Phi \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma} \sigma \Phi v \, d\Gamma = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\) \cite{ref3}, where \(\alpha\) is the diffusion coefficient, \(\Phi\) is the solution variable, \(v\) is the test function, \(\sigma\) enforces boundary conditions, \(f\) is the source term, \(\Omega\) is the domain, and \(\Gamma\) is the boundary.

The mixed finite element method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods. These extra fields are constrained using Lagrange multipliers, enabling efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity. The method results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices, requiring specialized solvers. For instance, the mixed formulation of an elliptic PDE can be represented as: \(\begin{cases} \mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{B}^T \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{f} \\ \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} \end{cases}\), where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the stiffness matrix, \(\mathbf{u}\) is the displacement field, \(\mathbf{B}\) is the constraint matrix, \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is the Lagrange multiplier, and \(\mathbf{f}\) is the source term. This formulation prevents numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields, ensuring stability and accuracy where primal methods fail due to rank deficiency \cite{ref1}.

\section{Verification, Validation, and Best Practices}

Verification and validation of discretization methods are crucial steps in ensuring the accuracy and reliability of numerical simulations. The choice of discretization method, such as the primal finite element method, directly affects the outcome of the simulation. Primal finite element methods solve partial differential equations without additional fields, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables, as evidenced by the equation \(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\) \cite{ref1}. However, these methods face challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. Discretization involves approximating the weak form using piecewise polynomial basis functions over a mesh, transforming the infinite-dimensional problem into a finite system of equations \cite{ref3}. This process requires careful handling of boundary conditions and material properties.

When formulating and solving saddle point systems, best practices emphasize the importance of careful handling of constraints and additional fields. In contrast, the mixed finite element method introduces additional fields to solve PDEs, constrained using Lagrange multipliers, enabling efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity.
