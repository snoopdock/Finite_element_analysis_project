\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method}
\label{sec:b4de2d7e-2034-4c5c-b382-06f2fd93965e}

The Galerkin method is a foundational approach in the Finite Element Method (FEM) for solving differential equations, introduced by Boris Galerkin. This technique involves approximating the solution of a problem by projecting it onto a finite-dimensional space of basis functions. The method ensures that the residual of the differential equation is orthogonal to the chosen basis functions, leading to a system of algebraic equations. Mathematically, this is represented by the weak form equation \(\int_{\Omega} w(x) \left( \frac{d^2 u}{dx^2} + f(x) \right) dx = 0\), for all \(w(x) \in V\). This formulation reduces the smoothness requirements for the approximate solution, making the Galerkin method a powerful tool for solving partial differential equations.

The core of the Finite Element Method lies in the weak formulation and assembly of the problem. The weak form is obtained by multiplying the strong form of the governing differential equation with a test function and integrating over the domain, ensuring that the solution satisfies the equation in an integral sense. This step reduces the smoothness requirements for the approximate solution, making it more tractable for numerical simulations. The Galerkin method is then applied by projecting the solution onto a finite-dimensional space of basis functions, resulting in a system of algebraic equations. The assembly of local stiffness and mass matrices into global system matrices is crucial to ensure the problem is mathematically well-posed and numerically stable.

The use of curved elements in the Finite Element Method (FEM) significantly enhances accuracy in complex geometries by allowing for more precise modeling of domains with curved boundaries. This is achieved through the use of higher-order polynomial shapes, which reduce discretization errors compared to straight-edged elements. The incorporation of curved elements is particularly critical in weak Galerkin methods, where they enhance the flexibility of the solution space, thereby improving the overall accuracy of the numerical solution. By mapping local coordinates \((\xi, \eta)\) to global coordinates \(x\) using shape functions \(N_i\) and control points \(X_i\), curved elements enable accurate modeling of complex geometries, as demonstrated by the equation \(x(\xi, \eta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} N_i(\xi, \eta) X_i\).

\section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}
\label{sec:437252bc-b540-4157-9bd6-86f677222428}

The Finite Element Method (FEM) is a powerful numerical technique for solving partial differential equations (PDEs). A foundational approach in FEM is the Galerkin method, introduced by Boris Galerkin, which involves approximating the solution of a problem by projecting it onto a finite-dimensional space of basis functions, ensuring that the residual of the differential equation is orthogonal to the chosen basis functions \cite{src:wiki_boris_galerkin-074a16ec4ded}. This method forms the theoretical basis for modern FEM formulations. Furthermore, FEM can be applied to complex geometries using curved elements, which allow for more accurate modeling of domains with curved boundaries by using higher-order polynomial shapes \cite{src:arxiv_2210.16907v1-63943b76987f}.

The assembly and solution of the finite element equations are crucial steps in the Finite Element Method. The local element equations are assembled into a global system of algebraic equations, which is then solved using numerical methods such as direct solvers or iterative techniques. For limit analysis, optimization algorithms replace traditional solvers to compute collapse loads directly, as shown in the optimization problem \(\min_{\mathbf{\sigma}} \int_{\Omega} \mathbf{\sigma} : \mathbf{\varepsilon} d\Omega \quad \text{subject to } \mathbf{\sigma} \in K\) \cite{src:wiki_finite_element_limit_analysis-ee8329dff8dd}. The choice of solver depends on problem size, sparsity, and desired accuracy, with iterative methods favored for their memory efficiency \cite{src:arxiv_1512.02390v2-fb5a8318bf1c}.

The Finite Element Method (FEM) has been extended to incorporate high-order and domain decomposition techniques to improve its accuracy and efficiency. High-order basis functions, such as those used in the Spectral Element Method (SEM), enable exponential error decay, reducing the number of degrees of freedom required for smooth solutions. This is particularly beneficial for high-frequency wave simulations. Furthermore, domain decomposition methods, such as the Additive Schwarz Method, allow for the division of the computational domain into subdomains, improving convergence for elliptic problems. The method's nonuniform weighting, based on smoothed sign functions, enhances performance for spectral element solvers. The residual reduction formula, \(\frac{\|r_{k+1}\|}{\|r_k\|} \leq C \cdot \exp(-\gamma \sqrt{\frac{N}{\log N}})\), quantifies the exponential convergence of SEM with increasing polynomial order \cite{src:arxiv_1512.02390v2-fb5a8318bf1c}.
