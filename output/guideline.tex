\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method (merged with The Finite Element Procedure)}

The weak formulation plays a pivotal role in problem formulation, allowing for the conversion of governing partial differential equations (PDEs) into a variational form. This is achieved by multiplying the PDEs with test functions and integrating over the domain, thereby enabling the handling of irregular geometries and discontinuous coefficients. The resulting weak form ensures compatibility with numerical discretization and enforces boundary conditions weakly. It forms the basis for assembling the global stiffness matrix, as demonstrated in the variational equation \(a(u, v) = L(v)\), where \(u\) is the solution in the Hilbert space \(V\), \(v\) is a test function, and \(L(v)\) represents the action of the linear functional.

The discretization of the weak form is achieved by approximating it using piecewise polynomial basis functions over a mesh, transforming the infinite-dimensional problem into a finite system of equations. The choice of basis functions affects accuracy and computational cost, and careful handling of boundary conditions and material properties is required. Céa's Lemma provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing errors in finite element methods applied to elliptic partial differential equations. The lemma relies on the properties of continuity and coercivity of the bilinear form, ensuring the existence and uniqueness of solutions. \section{Rules for Modeling Physical Phenomena with FEM}

The Primal Finite Element Method relies on direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields, resulting in a simpler but less robust approach for certain physical phenomena. This method is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems. However, it faces challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. The primal approach is less suitable for problems requiring efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems, such as almost incompressible elasticity.

The assembly of the stiffness matrix \(K\) is a critical step in the Finite Element Method (FEM), as it encodes the system's physical properties and governs the solution's behavior. The matrix is assembled by summing element contributions, where each entry \(K_{ij}\) corresponds to the integral of the product of gradients of basis functions \(N_i\) and \(N_j\) over element \(\Omega_e\). This process involves computing element-level integrals and assembling them into a global system of equations, which couples degrees of freedom across the domain \cite{ref7}. The choice of basis functions, such as high-degree polynomials, affects the conditioning of the stiffness matrix and the efficiency of numerical integration \cite{ref7}.

In contrast to the Primal Finite Element Method, the Mixed Finite Element Method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, constrained using Lagrange multipliers to enable efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity. This method results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices, requiring specialized solvers. The mixed formulation prevents numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields, ensuring stability and accuracy where primal methods fail due to rank deficiency \cite{ref6}.

\section{Verification, Validation, and Best Practices}

Verification and validation are crucial steps in the finite element method, ensuring that the numerical solution accurately represents the physical phenomenon being modeled. Céa's Lemma provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing errors in finite element methods, establishing that the discrete solution approximates the true solution in a Hilbert space. However, certain physical phenomena, such as incompressible materials, pose challenges to primal finite element methods, which solve PDEs without additional fields. In these cases, mixed finite element methods, which introduce additional fields constrained using Lagrange multipliers, offer a more robust approach, as evident from the mixed formulation: \(\begin{cases} \mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{B}^T \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{f} \\ \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} \end{cases}\) \cite{ref6}.

The mixed finite element method is a crucial approach for implementing finite element methods, particularly in problems involving incompressible materials. By introducing additional fields to solve partial differential equations, mixed formulations decouple stress and strain fields, preventing numerical locking and ensuring stability and accuracy. This is achieved through the use of Lagrange multipliers, which constrain the additional fields and result in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices. Hybrid methods further refine this by enforcing continuity weakly through boundary Lagrange fields, reducing degrees of freedom and eliminating negative pivots from mixed formulations. As a result, mixed elements are essential for modeling incompressible materials, and hybrid methods provide an efficient and robust approach for solving such problems.

Analyzing error estimates and convergence rates using Céa's Lemma is crucial for verifying the accuracy of finite element solutions. By leveraging the properties of continuity and coercivity of the bilinear form, Céa's Lemma ensures the existence and uniqueness of solutions, thereby facilitating the analysis of convergence rates. Furthermore, the choice of basis functions, such as high-degree polynomials, significantly impacts the efficiency of numerical integration and the conditioning of the stiffness matrix, as discussed in \cite{ref7}. This is particularly relevant when employing high-degree polynomial basis functions, which enable exponential error reduction with increasing polynomial order, as demonstrated in \cite{ref1}.

\section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}

The Primal Finite Element Method is a foundation for direct discretization, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields. This approach is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems. The method faces challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. For instance, the mixed finite element method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods, and results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices. This is particularly useful in problems requiring efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity, where mixed finite elements prevent numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields.

Céa's Lemma provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing errors in finite element methods applied to elliptic partial differential equations. It establishes that the discrete solution in a finite-dimensional subspace approximates the true solution in a Hilbert space. The lemma relies on the properties of continuity and coercivity of the bilinear form, ensuring the existence and uniqueness of solutions. This concept is critical for proving convergence and stability of finite element approximations. According to Céa's Lemma, the error between the discrete and true solutions is bounded by the difference between the discrete and true solutions, which is a function of the mesh size and the polynomial order. The trade-off between mesh size and polynomial order is a crucial aspect of finite element methods, as higher polynomial orders reduce discretization error more rapidly than mesh refinement, but may increase computational costs and numerical instability \cite{ref1}.

The advanced finite element methods, such as the Mixed Finite Element Method and Loubignac Iteration, offer improved solutions for complex physical phenomena. The Mixed Finite Element Method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods, and results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices, requiring specialized solvers \cite{ref6}. This approach is particularly useful in problems requiring smooth stress distributions, such as in solid mechanics, where Loubignac Iteration can iteratively refine solutions to satisfy equilibrium and compatibility conditions, contributing to improving the accuracy of stress approximations in finite element analysis \cite{ref5}. By decoupling stress and strain fields, mixed finite elements prevent numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems, ensuring stability and accuracy where primal methods fail due to rank deficiency \cite{ref6}.
