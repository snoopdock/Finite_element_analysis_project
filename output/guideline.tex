\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method}

The formulation of weak forms is a crucial step in transforming partial differential equations (PDEs) into a weak form, which is essential for applying Galerkin's method and ensuring numerical stability. This process involves multiplying the governing PDEs with test functions and integrating over the domain, thereby reducing the smoothness requirements for the solution and enabling the use of piecewise basis functions. The resulting weak form is defined by the equation \(\int_{\Omega} \nabla u_h \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\) \cite{ref4}, \cite{ref1}, where \(u_h\) is the approximate solution, \(v\) is a test function, \(f\) is a source term, and \(\Omega\) is the domain. This weak formulation allows for solutions with lower regularity and is the basis for Finite Element Method (FEM) discretization.

Galerkin's method is a foundational technique in the Finite Element Method (FEM) that transforms partial differential equations into a weak form. It involves approximating the solution using a set of basis functions and minimizing the residual error over the domain. The weak form, defined by \(\int_{\Omega} \nabla u_h \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega \quad \forall v \in V_h\), allows for solutions with lower regularity and is the basis for FEM discretization. This approach ensures that the solution satisfies the governing equations in an integral sense rather than pointwise, and is critical for deriving variational formulations. The method is widely used in structural and electromagnetic problems, and is essential for ensuring numerical stability.

The accuracy of the Finite Element Method (FEM) solutions is quantified through error estimation, which analyzes the discrepancy between the exact solution and the numerical approximation. This process involves analyzing temporal and spatial discretization errors, such as \(O(\Delta t)\) for time steps and \(O(h^s)\) for spatial mesh sizes. The a-priori \(L^\infty\) assumption is often used to establish convergence guarantees, ensuring that the solution converges as the time step and mesh size approach zero. The convergence error bound is given by \(\|u - u_h\|_{L^\infty} \leq C(\Delta t + h^s)\), where \(u\) is the exact solution, \(u_h\) is the numerical solution, \(\Delta t\) is the time step, \(h\) is the mesh size, \(s\) is the polynomial order, and \(C\) is a constant. Discretization of the domain, achieved through mesh generation, is critical in quantifying numerical approximation errors, as a fine mesh improves accuracy but increases computational cost.

\section{Verification, Validation, and Best Practices}

Verification and validation techniques are crucial in ensuring the accuracy and reliability of Finite Element Method (FEM) solutions. According to the RULE: Mesh Refinement Strategy, mesh refinement must balance accuracy and computational cost, with local refinement in regions of high gradient or curvature improving solution quality, while coarse meshes in smooth regions reduce resource usage \cite{ref1}. Adaptive meshing techniques dynamically adjust element size based on error estimates, guiding the process of error estimation in FEM. This estimate guides adaptive mesh refinement and ensures solution accuracy.
