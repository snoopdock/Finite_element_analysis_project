\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method}

The formulation of the weak form involves transforming the original partial differential equation into an equivalent integral form by multiplying the equation with a test function and integrating over the domain. This process reduces the order of derivatives, making it feasible to use piecewise continuous functions for approximation. The weak form ensures compatibility between the discrete solution and the original equations, enabling the application of variational principles. It also facilitates the incorporation of boundary conditions into the numerical framework, as expressed by the weighted residual form of a PDE: \$ \textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Omega\} \textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\}\textasciicircum{}T \textbackslash{}mathbf\{L\}(\textbackslash{}mathbf\{u\}) d\textbackslash{}Omega = \textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Omega\} \textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\}\textasciicircum{}T \textbackslash{}mathbf\{f\} d\textbackslash{}Omega + \textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Gamma\} \textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\}\textasciicircum{}T \textbackslash{}mathbf\{t\} d\textbackslash{}Gamma \$ [wiki\_finite\_element\_method], [wiki\_galerkin\_method].

The Galerkin method is a core technique in the finite element method that projects the residual of a differential equation onto a finite-dimensional space of test functions. This process ensures that the approximate solution satisfies the weak form of the equation. The Galerkin method is distinct from other methods like the Ritz method due to its use of test functions independent of the trial functions. Mathematically, this is represented by the equation \$\textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Omega\} \textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\}\textasciicircum{}T \textbackslash{}mathbf\{R\}(\textbackslash{}mathbf\{u\}\_h) d\textbackslash{}Omega = 0\$ for all \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\} \textbackslash{}in V\_h\$ [wiki\_galerkin\_method]. The Galerkin method is foundational for deriving discrete equations and is closely tied to variational principles, as demonstrated by Boris Galerkin [wiki\_boris\_galerkin].

\section{The Finite Element Procedure: Discretization and Mesh Generation}

The discretization of the domain is a fundamental step in the finite element procedure, where the physical domain is divided into smaller, simpler subdomains called elements. This process, known as meshing, defines the geometry and connectivity of elements. The choice of element type (e.g., triangular, quadrilateral) depends on the problem's complexity, and a fine mesh improves accuracy but increases computational cost, requiring a balance between precision and efficiency. The quality of the mesh directly impacts the accuracy of the solution, and for complex geometries, curved elements or higher-order interpolation functions are used to improve approximation.

Mesh generation techniques play a crucial role in the finite element method, as they directly impact the accuracy of the solution. The mesh must conform to the geometry of the physical system, with element sizes adjusted to balance accuracy and computational cost. For complex geometries, curved elements or higher-order interpolation functions are used to improve approximation. The quality of the mesh is critical, requiring careful consideration of element types and distribution. This is evident in the use of spectral element methods, which employ high-degree polynomial basis functions to achieve higher accuracy per element, often leading to exponential convergence rates for smooth solutions.

The convergence of the discrete solution is a critical aspect of the finite element method, as it directly impacts the accuracy of the results. The choice of mesh and discretization method plays a crucial role in achieving convergence, with the mesh convergence being a key factor. According to the rule of mesh convergence, the mesh must be sufficiently refined to capture solution gradients, and excessive refinement increases computational cost. This is supported by the fact that a coarse mesh may lead to inaccurate results, while a refined mesh ensures that the numerical solution approaches the true solution. The finite element space, defined as \$ V\_h \textbackslash{}subset H\textasciicircum{}1(\textbackslash{}Omega) \$, is a discrete approximation of the continuous solution space, constructed using basis functions defined over a mesh. The choice of basis functions influences the accuracy and convergence of the method, and the subspace approximation is used to reduce the infinite-dimensional problem to a finite system of equations.

\section{The Finite Element Procedure: Weak Formulation and Algebraic Equations}

The weak form of Maxwell's equations is a crucial concept in the finite element procedure, representing the integral formulation of these equations. It is used to approximate solutions for electromagnetic problems by transforming differential equations into algebraic systems. The weak form is derived by multiplying the original partial differential equation with a test function and integrating over the domain, ensuring compatibility between the discrete solution and the original equations. This process reduces the order of derivatives, making it feasible to use piecewise continuous functions for approximation. The weak form of Maxwell's equations is given by \$ \textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Omega\} (\textbackslash{}mathbf\{E\} \textbackslash{}cdot \textbackslash{}nabla \textbackslash{}times \textbackslash{}mathbf\{H\}\textasciicircum{}*) \textbackslash{}, d\textbackslash{}Omega = \textbackslash{}int\_\{\textbackslash{}Omega\} \textbackslash{}mathbf\{J\} \textbackslash{}cdot \textbackslash{}mathbf\{H\}\textasciicircum{}* \textbackslash{}, d\textbackslash{}Omega \$, as stated in [wiki\_computational\_electromagnetics].

The derivation of algebraic equations from the weak formulation of the problem involves the application of the Galerkin method. This process projects the weak form onto a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by basis functions \$ \textbackslash{}phi\_i \$, resulting in a system of algebraic equations where the coefficients \$ u\_i \$ are determined by satisfying the weak form for each basis function \$ \textbackslash{}phi\_j \$, ensuring consistency with the original PDE. The Galerkin projection is mathematically represented as \$ \textbackslash{}sum\_\{i=1\}\textasciicircum{}N u\_i B(\textbackslash{}phi\_i, \textbackslash{}phi\_j) = \textbackslash{}langle f, \textbackslash{}phi\_j \textbackslash{}rangle \textbackslash{}quad \textbackslash{}text\{for \} j = 1, \textbackslash{}dots, N \$, as described in the spectral element method [wiki\_spectral\_element\_method]. This step is crucial in transforming the weak form into a discrete system of equations, which can be solved numerically to obtain the solution.

The assembly and solution of the global system is a critical step in the finite element procedure. Local element equations are assembled into a global system by summing contributions from all elements, ensuring compatibility between elements and enforcing boundary conditions at the global level. This process involves computing stiffness matrices and load vectors for individual elements, which are then combined into a global stiffness matrix and load vector. The global system of equations is solved using numerical techniques, such as Gaussian elimination or iterative solvers, to obtain the unknown coefficients of the basis functions. \section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}

Variational principles form the mathematical foundation of the Finite Element Method (FEM), involving the minimization of a functional derived from the governing differential equation. This approach transforms the problem into finding a function that satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation. The roots of variational methods trace back to the 17th century with problems like the brachistochrone, as noted in the historical context of FEM. The weak form of a partial differential equation is derived by multiplying the equation by a test function and integrating over the domain, reducing the order of derivatives and enabling the use of piecewise continuous functions for approximation. This process ensures compatibility between the discrete solution and the original equations, facilitating the application of variational principles. The Galerkin method, a core technique in FEM, projects the residual of a differential equation onto a finite-dimensional space of test functions, ensuring that the approximate solution satisfies the weak form of the equation. The bilinear form in the Galerkin method computes the stiffness matrix by integrating the product of strain-displacement matrices and material properties over each element, linking the physical properties of the material to the numerical approximation.

Piecewise polynomial approximation is a fundamental concept in the Finite Element Method (FEM), where solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs) are approximated using piecewise polynomial functions over a discretized domain. These functions are defined on elements (e.g., triangles, quadrilaterals) and ensure continuity across element boundaries, allowing for flexible modeling of complex geometries and varying material properties. The versatility of FEM stems from its ability to handle irregular domains and non-uniform meshes, as demonstrated in [s2\_2a514cd276e01e493d923e733c3aedda0556381b].
