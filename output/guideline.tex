Furthermore, enrichment functions in XFEM can be used to capture discontinuities like cracks or material interfaces without requiring mesh alignment, reducing computational costs. The selection of enrichment functions must be based on the nature of the discontinuity or singularity, such as Heaviside functions for cracks or asymptotic crack-tip functions for stress singularities.

\section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}
\label{sec:0557ce05-c337-4bc8-9d53-8f4f1a870fe6}

Céa's Lemma is a fundamental result in the analysis of the finite element method (FEM) for elliptic partial differential equations. It provides a framework for proving error estimates by relating the exact solution of a variational problem to its finite-dimensional approximation. The lemma relies on the properties of Hilbert spaces, bilinear forms, and bounded linear operators. Mathematically, Céa's Lemma is expressed as \(a(u, v) = L(v)\) for all \(v \in V\), where \(V\) is a Hilbert space with norm \(\| \cdot \|\) and \(a: V \times V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is a bilinear form satisfying continuity and coercivity conditions \cite{src:wiki_c_a_s_lemma-c997aed28ad6}. This lemma ensures that the finite element solution converges to the true solution under appropriate conditions, such as continuity and stability of the bilinear form.

The weak formulation is a pivotal concept in the finite element method, enabling the transformation of partial differential equations into integral forms suitable for numerical approximation. By multiplying the strong form with test functions and integrating over the domain, the weak formulation relaxes the differentiability requirements of the solution, allowing for piecewise continuous approximations. This approach ensures compatibility with numerical discretization and enables the use of variational principles. The weak form is typically expressed as a bilinear equation involving a stress tensor, a body force, and a traction on the boundary, as given by the equation \(B(u, v) = \langle f, v \rangle \quad \text{for all } v \in V\) \cite{src:wiki_babu_ka_lax_milgram_theorem-a334b87f26cc}. This formulation is central to the finite element method, as it allows for the derivation of local equations using variational principles or weighted residual methods, which are then expressed in terms of nodal degrees of freedom.

Mesh refinement techniques are a crucial aspect of the finite element method (FEM), as they directly impact the accuracy and reliability of numerical solutions. The accuracy of FEM heavily depends on mesh quality, and distorted elements can lead to numerical errors. To address this issue, techniques like the Stretched Grid Method (SGM) can be employed. SGM generates pseudo-regular meshes by minimizing potential energy associated with grid segment lengths, thereby reducing element distortion. This is achieved by minimizing the potential energy functional, \(\Pi = D \sum_{j=1}^{n} R_j^2\), where \(D\) is a constant, \(R_j\) is the length of segment \(j\), and \(n\) is the total number of segments \cite{src:wiki_stretched_grid_method-c5b6e6e4f51e}. By improving mesh quality, SGM enables more accurate stress-strain predictions and reduces the risk of ill-conditioned stiffness matrices.
