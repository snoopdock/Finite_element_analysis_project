\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method}

Variational principles form the mathematical foundation of the Finite Element Method (FEM), involving the minimization of a functional derived from the governing differential equation. This approach transforms the problem into finding a function that satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation. The weak form of partial differential equations is a crucial step in the finite element procedure, which involves transforming the strong form of a partial differential equation into an equivalent integral form. The weak form is typically expressed as a bilinear equation involving the solution and test functions, as shown in \$B(u, v) = \textbackslash{}langle f, v \textbackslash{}rangle \textbackslash{}quad \textbackslash{}text\{for all \} v \textbackslash{}in V\$. This equation represents the weighted residual form of a PDE, where \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{L\}(\textbackslash{}mathbf\{u\})\$ is the differential operator applied to the solution \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{u\}\$, \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{w\}\$ are test functions, \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{f\}\$ is the body force, and \$\textbackslash{}mathbf\{t\}\$ is the traction on the boundary \$\textbackslash{}Gamma\$. The weak form allows for less restrictive continuity requirements on the solution compared to the strong form.

The Galerkin method is a core technique in the Finite Element Method (FEM) that projects the residual of a differential equation onto a finite-dimensional space of test functions. It ensures that the approximate solution satisfies the weak form of the equation. This method is foundational for deriving discrete equations and is closely tied to variational principles. This formulation enables the use of variational principles and ensures compatibility with numerical discretization, making it central to the finite element method.

The convergence analysis of the finite element procedure relies on the coercivity condition, which ensures the bilinear form \$B\$ is uniformly positive definite, guaranteeing the existence and uniqueness of the weak solution. This condition is mathematically represented as \$|B(u, u)| \textbackslash{}geq c \textbackslash{}|u\textbackslash{}|\textasciicircum{}2 \textbackslash{}quad \textbackslash{}text\{for some \} c > 0\$ [wiki\_babu\_ka\_lax\_milgram\_theorem]. Furthermore, mesh refinement near gradients is a critical rule to prevent numerical errors and ensure convergence, particularly in problems involving stress concentrations, fluid flow, or electromagnetic fields where localized effects dominate [s2\_2a514cd276e01e493d923e733c3aedda0556381b]. By refining the mesh near singularities, such as cracks or sharp corners, the accuracy of the solution can be significantly improved, as poor mesh refinement can lead to substantial errors in stress or strain predictions [wiki\_spectral\_element\_method].

The solution of the linear system in the Galerkin method is obtained by applying the Galerkin projection, which projects the weak form onto a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by basis functions \$\textbackslash{}phi\_i\$. The system of equations is then solved using direct or iterative methods to obtain the unknown coefficients of the basis functions. The solution is then post-processed to compute derived quantities like stresses, strains, or fluxes at desired locations.
