\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method}

The formulation of weak forms is a crucial step in transforming partial differential equations (PDEs) into a weak form, which is essential for applying Galerkin's method and ensuring numerical stability. According to the PROCEDURE: Formulation of Weak Forms, the governing PDEs are converted into a weak (variational) form by multiplying with test functions and integrating over the domain. This step reduces the smoothness requirements for the solution and enables the use of piecewise basis functions. The weak form is defined by the equation \(\int_{\Omega} \nabla u_h \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\), where \(u_h\) is the approximate solution, \(v\) is a test function, \(f\) is a source term, and \(\Omega\) is the domain. This process is critical for handling complex boundary conditions and nonlinear problems, as it ensures that the solution satisfies the equations in an integral sense rather than pointwise.

Galerkin's method plays a pivotal role in transforming partial differential equations into a weak form, which is essential for ensuring numerical stability and deriving variational formulations. By approximating the solution using a set of basis functions and minimizing the residual error over the domain, Galerkin's method ensures that the solution satisfies the governing equations in an integral sense rather than pointwise. This approach is critical for deriving weak forms of PDEs, which can be expressed as \(\int_{\Omega} \nabla u_h \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega \quad \forall v \in V_h\) [wiki\_boris\_galerkin, arxiv\_1712.01987v1]. The weak form allows for solutions with lower regularity and is the basis for FEM discretization, enabling the use of piecewise basis functions and reducing the smoothness requirements for the solution.

The finite element procedure involves formulating weak equations, which is a crucial step in transforming partial differential equations into a weak form. This process accommodates discontinuous fields and natural boundary conditions. For mixed methods, introducing additional variables, such as pressure or flux, into the weak form is essential. The mixed finite element method, for instance, introduces Lagrange multipliers to enforce continuity or physical constraints, resulting in a saddle-point system with non-definite matrices. This approach is particularly effective for problems like almost incompressible elasticity, where standard methods may fail. The hybrid finite element method, on the other hand, uses Lagrange multipliers on element boundaries to weakly enforce continuity, eliminating the need for shared degrees of freedom between elements. According to the RULE: Use Mixed Methods for Incompressible Materials, mixed finite element methods prevent numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling pressure and displacement fields.
