\section{The Finite Element Procedure: Discretization and Mesh Generation}

The discretization of the domain is a critical step in the finite element procedure, where the physical domain is divided into smaller, simpler subdomains called elements. This process, known as meshing, defines the geometry and connectivity of elements. The choice of element type (e.g., triangular, quadrilateral) depends on the problem's complexity, with a fine mesh improving accuracy but increasing computational cost. Discretization is the process of dividing a continuous domain into discrete elements to approximate solutions numerically, reducing complex partial differential equations to manageable algebraic equations.

The creation of a mesh that defines the geometry and connectivity of elements is a crucial step in the Finite Element Procedure. This mesh must conform to the geometry of the physical system, with element sizes adjusted to balance accuracy and computational cost. For complex geometries, curved elements or higher-order interpolation functions are used to improve approximation. The quality of the mesh directly impacts the accuracy of the solution, requiring careful consideration of element types and distribution.

Mesh convergence is a critical aspect of the finite element method, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the results. The mesh must be sufficiently refined to capture solution gradients, as coarse meshes may lead to inaccurate results, while excessive refinement increases computational cost. Convergence studies verify that numerical solutions approach the true solution as mesh density increases, thereby validating the accuracy of the finite element method [wiki\_finite\_element\_method]. This process is essential for handling complex geometries and varying material properties, as it allows localized refinement of the mesh to capture solution gradients accurately.

The convergence analysis of the finite element method is a critical step in ensuring the accuracy and reliability of numerical solutions. Through mesh refinement and discretization, the method approximates solutions by dividing the continuous domain into discrete elements. \section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}

Variational principles form the mathematical foundation of the Finite Element Method (FEM), involving the minimization of a functional derived from the governing differential equation. This approach transforms the problem into finding a function that satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation. The Galerkin method, a core technique in FEM, projects the residual of a differential equation onto a finite-dimensional space of test functions, ensuring that the approximate solution satisfies the weak form of the equation. The bilinear form in the Galerkin method computes the stiffness matrix by integrating the product of strain-displacement matrices and material properties over each element, linking the physical properties of the material to the numerical approximation. By approximating solutions to partial differential equations using piecewise polynomial functions over a discretized domain, FEM allows for flexible modeling of complex geometries and varying material properties.
