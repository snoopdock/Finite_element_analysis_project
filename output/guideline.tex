This is achieved by multiplying the PDEs with test functions and integrating over the domain, thereby enabling the handling of irregular geometries and discontinuous coefficients. The resulting weak form ensures compatibility with numerical discretization and enforces boundary conditions weakly. \section{Rules for Modeling Physical Phenomena with FEM}

The Primal Finite Element Method relies on direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields, resulting in a simpler but less robust approach for certain physical phenomena. This method is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems, as shown in equation \(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\) \cite{ref5}. However, primal finite element methods face challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel \cite{ref5}. As a result, mixed finite elements are preferred for incompressible materials to prevent numerical locking and ensure stability and accuracy \cite{ref5}.

The mixed finite element method offers several advantages over the primal finite element method, particularly in problems involving incompressibility or constraints. By introducing additional fields and using Lagrange multipliers to enforce constraints, mixed formulations can efficiently solve numerically ill-posed problems, such as almost incompressible elasticity. This is achieved through a saddle point system, which arises in mixed formulations and consists of a non-definite matrix structure. The mixed finite element method prevents numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields, ensuring stability and accuracy where primal methods fail due to rank deficiency.

\section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}

The Primal Finite Element Method is a foundation for direct discretization, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields. This approach is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems. However, primal finite element methods face challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. The limitations of primal methods are evident in their inability to prevent numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems, as opposed to mixed finite elements which decouple stress and strain fields.

Céa's Lemma provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing errors in finite element methods applied to elliptic partial differential equations. It establishes that the discrete solution in a finite-dimensional subspace approximates the true solution in a Hilbert space, relying on the properties of continuity and coercivity of the bilinear form. This concept is critical for proving convergence and stability of finite element approximations, as demonstrated by the mathematical framework: Let \(V\) be a Hilbert space with norm \(\|\cdot\|\). For a bilinear form \(a: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfying \(|a(v, w)| \leq \gamma \|v\| \|w\|\) (continuity) and \(a(v, v) \geq \alpha \|v\|^2\) (coercivity), and a bounded linear functional \(L: V \to \mathbb{R}\), the solution \(u \in V\) to \(a(u, v) = L(v)\) for all \(v \in V\) is approximated by \(u_h \in V_h\), a subspace of \(V\) \cite{ref8}.

The mixed finite element method is particularly useful in problems requiring smooth stress distributions, such as in solid mechanics, where it can prevent numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields. This is achieved by introducing additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods, and constrained using Lagrange multipliers. The resulting saddle point system, \(\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{K} & \mathbf{B}^T \\ \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u} \\ \boldsymbol{\lambda} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{f} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}\), arises in mixed formulations and requires specialized solvers. In such cases, Loubignac iteration can be employed to iteratively refine solutions and satisfy equilibrium and compatibility conditions, improving the accuracy of stress approximations in finite element analysis.
