\section{Mathematical Foundation: Strong Form, Weak Form, and Galerkin Method (merged with The Finite Element Procedure)}

The Primal Finite Element Method is a foundation for direct discretization, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields. This approach is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems, as described by the equation \(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\) \cite{ref4}. However, primal methods face challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel. The weak form, which is a crucial step in the discretization process, involves converting the governing partial differential equations into a weak (variational) form by multiplying with test functions and integrating over the domain, as shown in the equation \(\int_{\Omega} \alpha \nabla \Phi \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma} \sigma \Phi v \, d\Gamma = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\) \cite{ref7}.

The weak form of a partial differential equation (PDE) plays a pivotal role in the mathematical foundation of the finite element method. By converting the governing PDEs into a weak (variational) form through multiplication with test functions and integration over the domain, as described in the procedure of weak formulation \cite{ref7}, irregular geometries and discontinuous coefficients can be effectively handled. This weak form ensures compatibility with numerical discretization and enforces boundary conditions weakly, forming the basis for assembling the global stiffness matrix. For instance, the weak form of an elliptic PDE is given by \(\int_{\Omega} \alpha \nabla \Phi \cdot \nabla v \, d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma} \sigma \Phi v \, d\Gamma = \int_{\Omega} f v \, d\Omega\) \cite{ref7}, where \(\alpha\) is the diffusion coefficient, \(\Phi\) is the solution variable, \(v\) is the test function, \(\sigma\) enforces boundary conditions, \(f\) is the source term, \(\Omega\) is the domain, and \(\Gamma\) is the boundary.

The mixed finite element method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods. These extra fields are constrained using Lagrange multipliers, enabling efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity. The method results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices, requiring specialized solvers. For instance, the mixed formulation of an elliptic PDE can be represented as: \(\begin{cases} \mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{B}^T \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{f} \\ \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} \end{cases}\), where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the stiffness matrix, \(\mathbf{u}\) is the displacement field, \(\mathbf{B}\) is the constraint matrix, \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is the Lagrange multiplier, and \(\mathbf{f}\) is the source term. This formulation prevents numerical locking in nearly incompressible problems by decoupling stress and strain fields, ensuring stability and accuracy where primal methods fail due to rank deficiency \cite{ref4}.

\section{Introduction and Scope of the Finite Element Method}

The Primal Finite Element Method is a foundation for direct discretization, relying on the direct discretization of primary variables without additional fields. This approach is termed 'irreducible' and typically uses symmetric positive definite systems. The primal finite element method solves partial differential equations (PDEs) without additional fields, as seen in the equation \(\mathbf{K} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}\), where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the stiffness matrix, \(\mathbf{u}\) is the displacement vector, and \(\mathbf{f}\) is the force vector. However, this method faces challenges in problems with constraints or incompressibility, where mixed formulations excel, as stated in \cite{ref4}.

Céa's Lemma provides a theoretical foundation for analyzing errors in finite element methods applied to elliptic partial differential equations. It establishes that the discrete solution in a finite-dimensional subspace approximates the true solution in a Hilbert space. The lemma relies on the properties of continuity and coercivity of the bilinear form, ensuring the existence and uniqueness of solutions. This concept is critical for proving convergence and stability of finite element approximations. For instance, in the weak form of an elliptic PDE, the solution variable \(\Phi\) is approximated by \(u_h\) in a subspace \(V_h\), where the bilinear form \(a\) satisfies continuity and coercivity. This is crucial for establishing error bounds, as demonstrated by Céa's Lemma, which states that the error between the discrete solution \(u_h\) and the true solution \(u\) is bounded by the norm of the difference between the discrete and true solutions, weighted by the coercivity constant \(\alpha\). \cite{ref5}

The mixed finite element method introduces additional fields to solve partial differential equations, contrasting with primal methods. These extra fields are constrained using Lagrange multipliers, enabling efficient solutions for numerically ill-posed problems like almost incompressible elasticity. The method results in a saddle point system with non-definite matrices, requiring specialized solvers. This system arises in mixed formulations, where \(\mathbf{K}\) is the stiffness matrix, \(\mathbf{B}\) enforces constraints via Lagrange multipliers \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), and \(\mathbf{u}\) represents primary variables.
